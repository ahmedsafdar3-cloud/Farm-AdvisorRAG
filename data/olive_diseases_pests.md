# Olive Tree Diseases and Pests

## Olive Knot (Pseudomonas savastanoi)
Olive knot is a bacterial disease that produces rough, tumor-like galls on branches, twigs, and occasionally leaves. The bacteria enter through wounds caused by pruning cuts, frost cracks, or insect damage, most often during wet weather. Infected trees show reduced vigor and, in severe cases, dieback of affected limbs. Management relies on pruning out and destroying infected wood during dry weather, disinfecting tools between cuts, and avoiding pruning right before rain. Copper-based bactericides applied before autumn rains can reduce new infections but will not cure existing galls.

## Verticillium Wilt
Verticillium wilt is a soil-borne fungal disease that blocks the tree's vascular system, causing sudden wilting of leaves and branches, often on just one side of the canopy. Leaves may turn a dull grey-green and remain attached to dead twigs rather than dropping, which is a useful diagnostic sign. There is no chemical cure once a tree is infected. Prevention focuses on avoiding planting in fields previously used for cotton, tomato, or other susceptible crops, ensuring good drainage, and removing severely infected trees to reduce inoculum in the soil.

## Olive Fruit Fly (Bactrocera oleae)
This is the most economically damaging insect pest of olives worldwide. Females lay eggs inside the fruit, and the larvae tunnel through the flesh, causing premature fruit drop and creating entry points for secondary fungal rot. Damage peaks in late summer as fruit begins to ripen and sugar content rises. Monitoring with yellow sticky traps or pheromone traps starting in early summer allows growers to time interventions. Options include mass trapping, kaolin clay particle film sprays that deter egg-laying, and targeted bait sprays combining a protein attractant with a low-toxicity insecticide, applied only to trap-indicated hotspots rather than the whole orchard.

## Peacock Spot (Fusicladium oleagineum)
Peacock spot appears as circular dark olive-green to black spots on the upper leaf surface, often surrounded by a yellow halo, resembling a peacock feather eye. Severe infections cause premature leaf drop, weakening the tree and reducing next season's yield. The fungus thrives in cool, wet conditions, particularly autumn and spring rains. Copper-based fungicide applications in autumn before the rainy season, and again in spring, are the standard preventive measure. Good canopy ventilation through proper pruning reduces humidity within the tree and lowers infection pressure.

## Black Scale (Saissetia oleae)
Black scale is a sap-sucking insect that weakens trees and excretes honeydew, which promotes sooty mold growth on leaves and fruit, reducing photosynthesis and marketability. Populations build up especially in dense, poorly ventilated canopies. Natural enemies, including parasitic wasps, often keep populations in check if broad-spectrum insecticides are avoided. Where intervention is needed, horticultural oil sprays timed to target the young crawler stage are effective with minimal disruption to beneficial insects.

## General Early Detection Guidance
Weekly canopy inspection during the growing season, focusing on new growth and the undersides of leaves, catches most problems before they cause serious yield loss. Photographing symptoms and comparing against known disease patterns, combined with checking recent weather (extended wet periods increase fungal and bacterial risk), narrows down the likely cause quickly. When symptoms are ambiguous or spreading rapidly, sending a sample to a local agricultural extension office for lab confirmation is recommended before applying broad treatments.
