# Irrigation Management for Orchard Crops

## Water Requirements by Growth Stage
Water demand is not constant through the season. The critical periods for olives are flowering and fruit set in late spring, and pit hardening through oil accumulation in summer and early autumn. Deficit irrigation (deliberately reducing water below full crop demand) during pit hardening is a widely used technique to control excessive vegetative growth and can improve oil quality, but the same deficit applied during flowering or fruit set instead causes significant yield loss through fruit drop.

## Signs of Overwatering
Waterlogged soil around the root zone reduces oxygen availability, which damages fine roots and increases susceptibility to root rot pathogens such as Phytophthora. Visible symptoms include yellowing leaves that drop from the inside of the canopy outward, a general lack of vigor despite adequate nutrition, and in severe cases a sour smell in the soil. Improving drainage, reducing irrigation frequency while increasing the volume per session, and avoiding irrigation immediately after heavy rain all help correct this.

## Signs of Underwatering
Drought-stressed trees show leaf rolling or a dull grey-green cast to the canopy, premature fruit drop especially of smaller fruit, and in prolonged stress, leaf scorch starting at the tips and margins. Mature olive trees are notably drought tolerant compared to many orchard crops, but young trees in their first three years need consistent moisture to establish a deep root system and should not be subjected to significant deficit irrigation.

## Irrigation Scheduling Methods
Soil moisture sensors placed at two depths (shallow, roughly 20-30cm, and deep, roughly 50-60cm) give a much better picture than a fixed calendar schedule, since they account for actual rainfall and evapotranspiration. Where sensors are not available, tracking reference evapotranspiration (ET0) from a nearby weather station and applying a crop coefficient for the current growth stage gives a reasonable estimate of daily water need. As a simplified rule of thumb during peak summer demand, mature drip-irrigated olive trees typically need in the range of 30-50 liters per tree per day, adjusted up in extreme heat and down during cooler or rainy periods.

## Drip Irrigation System Checks
Emitter clogging from mineral deposits or algae is the most common cause of uneven irrigation in established drip systems. A quick monthly check of flow rate at several representative emitters, compared against the manufacturer's rated output, catches clogging before it causes visible plant stress. Flushing the system's main and sub-main lines each season, and using an inline filter appropriate to the water source (sand media for surface water, screen filters for cleaner well water), significantly extends emitter life.

## Water Quality Considerations
Irrigation water with high salinity or sodium content can, over years, cause soil structure degradation and salt accumulation in the root zone, particularly in drip systems where water is not periodically flushed through the full soil profile by rainfall. Periodic leaching irrigation, applying extra water beyond crop need to push accumulated salts below the root zone, is a standard corrective practice in regions using groundwater with moderate to high salinity.
